Virtual games for large groups: splitting a 30-person all-hands into rooms without chaos

Ahmed Moaz · 10 October 2026

Thirty people will not fit in one game, and they should not try. Multiplayer browser games have seat limits, usually far below thirty, and a quiz with thirty players would be a queue with a scoreboard. Virtual games for large groups work when the split is planned before the call, not improvised in the chat while everyone waits. This is how to split a large group into teams for a 30-person all-hands: do the arithmetic, name a captain for every room, publish a roster instead of a pile of links, and respect the clock on an empty room. I build Boardit, a small site of free online games, so its seat limits are the worked example.

Do the room arithmetic before the all-hands meeting

How many people per breakout room? Start from the game's seat limit, then subtract one. A room filled to its limit has nowhere for the person who joins late.

On Boardit, a quiz room holds two to eight, which is why its guide for teachers turns a class of 30 into four rooms of eight or five of six. Four rooms would mean 8, 8, 7 and 7. For games for 30 people I would take five rooms of six: every room then has two spare seats.

A game with a bigger seat limit needs fewer rooms and fewer captains, but in a turn-based game every extra player is another turn to wait through.

Smaller rooms also change how hard people try. In 1979, Latané, Williams and Harkins had people clap and shout alone and in groups, and found a sizeable drop in individual effort in groups. They called it social loafing. In a room of six, each player is a sixth of the game. In a room of thirty, nobody notices who stopped answering.

Name a room captain for every room

A captain is the one person in each room who opens the game, holds the code and starts on time. Pick them in advance and tell them in a direct message, not in the all-hands chat.

Give each captain three lines of instruction:

  1. Open the room only when your breakout starts, and choose the same settings as every other room.
  2. Switch the room to private before anyone joins. On Boardit's quiz, that switch has saved reliably since a fix on 29 September 2026; check it once anyway.
  3. Send the code to your own room's chat and nowhere else.

The third line matters more than it looks. Anyone who types a room code, or opens the invite link carrying it, takes a seat.

Virtual games for large groups need a roster, not a list of links

The tempting shortcut is one message in the all-hands chat with five invite links. Every one of those links works for all thirty people, so the fast clickers fill Room 1 and Room 5 sits empty.

Publish a roster instead: Room 1, then the captain's name, then five more names, and so on to Room 5. Number the rooms, put each captain first, and paste it into the agenda the day before. People find their own name, go to their breakout rooms and wait for their captain's code. Nobody needs to ask where to go, and no code leaves the room it belongs to.

Time the ten-minute buffer around the lobby

Leave ten minutes between the start of the breakout slot and the start of play. Nobody can time the joining in advance, and late arrivals are normal.

Boardit, as of October 2026, adds a reason to keep that buffer at the right end. An untouched lobby packs itself up after 10 minutes, but once a game is under way the room stays open for up to 12 hours. So a captain who opens the room at the start of the all-hands, forty minutes early, may find it gone. Open it when the breakout opens, not before.

A workable order for the slot:

  • Minute 0: breakouts open; each room captain opens a private room and posts the code.
  • Minutes 0 to 8: players join; latecomers take the spare seats.
  • Minute 8: every room starts, full or not.
  • Last five minutes: captains post their room's winner in the main chat.

Choose a game whose listed time fits what is left after the buffer, for the room size you picked.

One result without a shared scoreboard

Each room keeps its own score. Boardit's teacher guide says so plainly: separate rooms draw their own questions and keep their own scoreboards, with no combined table. Rooms that played different questions cannot be ranked against each other fairly, so do not pretend otherwise. Read out five winners, give each a line of applause, and stop. Virtual team building does not need a league table to work.

If the plan slips

Something will. A captain will be off sick, so name a reserve. Someone will land in the wrong room; with spare seats, let them stay. I have not run this plan with a real 30-person team, and the joining time is a guess with a margin rather than a measurement. Some people will not want to play at all, and remote team games at an all-hands should let them leave the breakout without explaining why.
